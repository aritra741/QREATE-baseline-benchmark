Once upon a time, there was a condition known as Trigeminal Neuralgia. This condition can be idiopathic, degenerative, vascular, traumatic, infectious, neoplastic, or due to other reasons. The most common causes of Trigeminal Neuralgia are the compression of the trigeminal nerve, tumors, pressure from blood vessels, aneurysms, multiple sclerosis, nerve injuries, shingles, post-herpetic neuralgia, trauma, infections, degeneration of teeth or jaw, and the aging process. Doctors diagnose this condition through clinical evaluations and imaging tests.

People with Trigeminal Neuralgia often experience unilateral facial pain, paroxysmal pain, sudden severe pain, sharp pain, stabbing pain, burning pain, shooting pain, electric shock-like pain, pain triggered by touch, eating, washing, shaving, brushing teeth, or talking. The pain intensifies over time and can spread to larger areas of the face, with some people also experiencing a background dull aching pain or continuous pain. They might involuntarily wince due to the intensity.

Treatment options for Trigeminal Neuralgia include oral medications, surgery, physical therapy, psychotherapy, and sometimes other methods. The prognosis varies; it can be a chronic condition, progressive, with remission possible, affecting quality of life in different ways, or highly variable and unknown in some cases. Pathogenesis can also be idiopathic, degenerative, vascular, traumatic, infectious viral, neoplastic, or due to other causes.

Managing Trigeminal Neuralgia is challenging due to potential drug side effects, treatment intolerance, insufficient evidence for many interventions, and the complexity of pain management.
