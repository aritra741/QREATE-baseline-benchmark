Once upon a time, there was a person grappling with Trigeminal Neuralgia. This condition could be caused by various factors such as the compression of trigeminal nerve, tumour, pressure from blood vessels, aneurysm, multiple sclerosis, nerve injury, shingles, post-herpetic neuralgia, trauma, infection, degeneration of teeth or jaw, and even the natural ageing process. To diagnose the condition, doctors rely on clinical evaluation and imaging techniques. Common symptoms include unilateral facial pain, paroxysmal pain, sudden severe pain, sharp pain, stabbing pain, burning pain, shooting pain, electric shock-like pain, pain triggered by touch, eating, washing, shaving, brushing teeth, or talking, with the intensity often increasing over time and spreading to larger areas of the face. Background dull aching pain and continuous pain could also be experienced. The condition can be treated through oral medication, surgery, physical therapy, psychotherapy, and other methods. However, the prognosis varies—it can be chronic, progressive, or have remission possibilities with varying degrees of quality of life impairment, and in some cases, it remains unknown. Pathogenesis could be idiopathic, degenerative, vascular, traumatic, infectious viral, neoplastic, or other. The challenges in treatment include dealing with drug side effects, managing treatment intolerance, insufficient evidence for many interventions, and the complexity of pain management.
