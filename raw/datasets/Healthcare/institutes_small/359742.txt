Digital data must be secured for personal privacy and protection from malicious intent. The regulatory framework and industry best practices for compliance are among the most significant components of the Internet because without these standards, private digital data could be used fraudulently. Most regulations and policies are intended to protect personal and financial information, and they define how data is organized, stored, transferred, and managed.
Data compliance is the activity of obeying the regulations and practices that safeguard data. Many organizations choose to exceed their legal obligations and develop policies and protocols that are far more strict.
Goals
Organizations set data compliance goals that align with regulations and standards, conforming to these rules to avoid fines for noncompliance. In the most extreme cases, executives could go to jail for noncompliance, but this is rare.
The underlying goals of data compliance include:
Protection from security breaches.
Safeguarding sensitive information from competitors.
Building the trust of clients and customers.
Guarding against data loss.
Building goodwill between the organization and regulatory bodies.
Promoting accountability within the organization.
Standards
The four compliance standards detailed below were selected because they are among the ones you are most likely to encounter. There are thousands more, however, so consider checking the regulatory framework specific to your industry and geographic area.
The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)is the most comprehensive data protection standard. It mandates that people have the right to know the data that organizations are retaining about them, and how the data can be used. In addition, this standard requires obtaining consent, defines the amount of data an entity may hold, clarifies the rights of individuals, and describes the protocols for reporting data breaches. Any entity that interacts with an individual subject to the EU’s jurisdiction is required to follow this standard.
The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPPA) is a U.S. law pertaining to how organizations with medical data ensure the confidentiality and security of those records. The Act requires that such documents may only be seen by persons with valid reasons for viewing them, and requires audit trails for every interaction with that data.
The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCIDSS) is an industry-based set of rules that protects customers’ financial information. Noncompliance can result in significant fines or termination of service with payment processors.
The California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) protects the privacy and applies to companies that (1) receive 50%+ of their revenue from the sale of customer information; (2) have gross revenues above $25 million; and (3) use or are the repositories of the personal information of at least 50,000 households, businesses, consumers, or devices. Organizations do not need to have a physical presence in California to be subject to this provision.
Best Practices for Compliance
Identify the personal data your organization holds and know how it is being used.
Secure personal data with a comprehensive strategy that guards against breaches and unauthorized disclosure during the data’s entire life cycle.
Document and review data protection policies, and conduct regular risk assessments.
Promptly comply with all applicable standards.
Assign tasks and responsibilities related to data protection.
Understand your organization’s ethical data privacy obligations to your customers and clients.
Create processes for handling customer complaints, deleting information, producing personal information reports, responding to data requests, and correcting data breaches.
Provide adequate training for your compliance program.
Summary
Failure to comply with data laws, regulations and standards can cost your organization dearly with tarnished public perception, fines and other punishments, and financial losses due to data breaches. Data compliance should be a top priority for every organization that handles sensitive customer data. Commit to reviewing your organization’s accountability and upgrade compliance procedures immediately.

Single Hypoglycemic Emergency Raises Risk of Death within 3 years for Diabetics, Says Study | The Diabetic News
Single Hypoglycemic Emergency Raises Risk of Death within 3 years for Diabetics, Says Study
One to two per 100 people with diabetes per year experience severe hypoglycemia ending in a hospital visit.
Alarming study showed that within three years of a dangerously low blood sugar episode requiring an emergency department visit, one-third of those in the study died. This is significant because each year, 1-2-percent of diabetics have a hypoglycemic event that leads to a hospital visit.
Additionally, using data from a large, longitudinal study, the researchers found that one third of the older adults with diabetes who had experienced a severe low blood sugar episode (hypoglycemia) died within three years of the incident.
In analyzing their data, the researchers controlled for such variables as how severe a person's diabetes was and how long it had been since diagnosis.
The researchers say that their findings suggest that doctors might want to pay special attention to patients who have been sent to the emergency department for hypoglycemia after losing consciousness, having a seizure or experiencing another serious health event. The findings were presented March 10 at the American Heart Association's EPI|LIFESTYLE 2017 Scientific Sessions in Portland, Ore.
"If you have a patient with a history of severe hypoglycemia, this could portend poorly for his or her future," says Alexandra K. Lee, MSPH, a PhD candidate in epidemiology at the Bloomberg School. "Our thinking has been that you resolve a hypoglycemic episode and it's over. But what this tells us is that one episode may have long-lasting consequences."
Says the study's senior author, Elizabeth Selvin, PhD, a professor of epidemiology at the School: "Hypoglycemia is clearly an under-recognized risk factor for death and cardiovascular disease in people with diabetes. We are treating many people in this country for high blood sugar. They need to be very careful that their treatment doesn't go too far and cause hypoglycemia, a potentially more serious condition than we have truly understood."
Diabetes is a condition marked by the inability of the body to properly manage the level of glucose (sugar) in the blood. Poorly controlled blood sugar – when blood sugar levels are too high – can lead to future complications such as blindness and nerve damage.
According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, roughly 29 million Americans have diabetes and other estimates say that roughly 85 percent are taking medication to lower their blood sugar.
Sometimes, those efforts to lower blood sugar with medication go too far. One to two per 100 people with diabetes per year experience severe hypoglycemia ending in a hospital visit, Lee says.
For the study, the researchers analyzed data from participants in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) cohort, a study of 15,792 residents in four communities in the United States, who were between the ages of 45 and 64 when the study began in 1987. Specifically, the researchers focused on 1,198 participants with type 2 diabetes, who were followed for an average of 15 years.
They found that 192 experienced hypoglycemia that led to an emergency room visit or hospitalization. Those people were nearly twice as likely to die of any cause than those people with diabetes who did not have severe hypoglycemia.
Researchers say that past studies have shown that people who experience severe hypoglycemia experience low-level heart damage, so a biological link between hypoglycemia and death from cardiovascular disease could be an extension of that. Additionally, the researchers say it isn't clear whether people who suffer from a hypoglycemic episode are already sicker than those who do not, or if the episode is what sickens them.
Diabetes medications like metformin, which are designed to lower high blood sugar, can be valuable in some patients, Selvin says, but they are not the "miracle" treatment that statins tend to be for people with high cholesterol. If diabetes medications are used improperly, blood sugar can get dangerously low.
At the same time, the benefits to small arteries of lowering blood sugar are often not realized for decades. Considering the risks of hypoglycemia, Selvin says that many older adults may see a downside and minimal upside to being on these diabetes medications.
"If the benefit is 10 or 20 years down the line and your patients are in their 70s, maybe we shouldn't be putting them on anti-diabetes medications," Selvin says. "It's something to consider."

Biotechnology Dispute Is Rekindled - NYTimes.com
Biotechnology Dispute Is Rekindled
A provocative footnote in a Federal court decision earlier this month has reignited a dispute over who really pioneered the method for producing new substances through genetic engineering.
The decision could undermine basic biotechnology patents held by the Genentech Corporation, opening the door to legal challenges to one of the industry's leaders, according to patent law experts.
Officials at Genentech in South San Francisco said they are aware of the ruling but have no comment yet about the possible impact on the company. Patent Application Denied
The decision on Aug. 10 by the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, the nation's highest patent court, rejected the patent application of scientists at the University of California who claimed to have made the discovery. The court ruled against the scientists on the basis of a legal technicality. But in discussing the history of the research, the court essentially agreed that the scientists were the true pioneers.
The court suggested in a footnote that a very broad patent was awarded last fall to Genentech for a discovery based on the work done at the University of California. The footnote could provide an opening for Genentech's competitors to challenge the patent.
Genentech's patent is so broad that, in theory, almost every company trying to make drugs through recombinant DNA technology needs a license from Genentech. Watershed Case
''The decision will probably be a watershed case in determining the obviousness of biotechnology inventions,'' said S. Leslie Misrock, a patent attorney in New York who represents many companies involved in biotechnology. ''Personally, I think it will have a significant impact on Genentech's Itakura patent.'' The company's patent is named after one of its researchers, Keiichi Itakura.
Patent experts also said that the full implications of the decision depend on how Genentech tries to use its patent. The company has publicly said it will grant nonexclusive licenses to other firms and that it will surrender all royalties to a charitable foundation that will finance new scientific research.
Thus far, however, the firm has not disclosed the terms it will impose on licensees. Officials at other biotechnology companies are worried, moreover, that Genentech could simply refuse to grant licenses to companies producing drugs that compete directly with its own. Drug to Dissolve Clots
For example, Genentech says it generated $100 million in sales on tissue plasminogen activator, a blood-clot-dissolving drug, in the five months after it received marketing approval last November from the Food and Drug Administration. But Burroughs-Wellcome, a subsidiary of Wellcome P.L.C. of Britain, is in clinical trials with its own version of the drug, and the two are locked in lawsuits over patent infringement. Genentech has two specific patents on the drug itself, but it can also cite its more basic claim to strengthen its case.
The rulings could ultimately affect an array of new drugs, including anti-cancer drugs like interferon and Interleukin-2. These and other biotechnology-based drugs are just beginning to receive Federal approval for marketing.
Biotechnology firms are still assessing the implications of the ruling, known in legal terms as ''In Re Patrick H. O'Farrell, Barry A. Polisky and David H. Gelfand.'' The three were the scientists at the University of California involved in the earlier work. 'Far-Reaching Impact'
''The court recognized that what they did was pioneering, that it actually predated the work that Genentech did,'' Albert Halluin said of the university researchers. He is chief intellectual property counsel for the Cetus Corporation of Emeryville, Calif.
''The case will certainly have a far-reaching impact,'' Mr. Halluin said.
The dispute goes back to 1973 when biotechnology was in its infancy and Stanley Cohen of Stanford University and Herbert Boyer of the University of California successfully inserted genes from a toad into bacteria. Genes are segments of DNA that carry the instructions for creating the individual proteins that make up a living organism. The Cohen-Boyer discovery opened the prospect of breeding bacteria that could serve as living factories for human protein.
One major hurdle, however, was that researchers still had not figured out how to make a transplanted human gene begin producing proteins. Missing Link
In 1976 a team led by Mr. Polisky and Mr. Gelfand at the University of California reported what appeared to be the missing link. Writing in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, they reported their success at getting bacteria to produce ribosomal RNA - a key step in reading genetic codes. The researchers also predicted that their method would probably yield more commercially valuable proteins.
They confirmed this prediction a few months later, but did not publish a second article until 1978, when the university applied for a patent.
The Patent and Trademark Office rejected the application on the ground that the scientists' 1976 article laid out the entire blueprint for producing proteins and that actually producing them had been obvious from their own published work. Under Federal patent law, inventors may wait one year after publishing their results before applying for a patent. After that deadline, however, an invention is considered public knowledge and unpatentable. The patent office said the scientists had made their real discovery in 1976 and had waited too long to apply for a patent.
